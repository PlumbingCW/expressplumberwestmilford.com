Electric Tankless Water Heaters on a 20 Amp Circuit

By the West Milford Express Plumbing team

A 20 amp circuit supports only a small point-of-use unit. Here is the math, and what a whole house needs instead.

People often ask whether a tankless water heater can run on an existing 20 amp circuit. For a whole-house unit the answer is no. For a small point-of-use unit at a single sink, the answer is yes.

The reason is simple arithmetic about heat, and cold winter water makes it harder.

The electrical limit

A 20 amp, 240 volt circuit can carry a continuous load of about 16 amps, which is roughly 3,800 watts. That is the ceiling for a small electric unit.

Whole-house electric tankless heaters often draw many times that, and need heavy circuits and sometimes a service upgrade.

What 3.5 kilowatts can heat

Heating water takes about 500 BTU per hour for each gallon per minute and each degree of temperature rise. One kilowatt supplies about 3,412 BTU per hour, so one kilowatt raises one gallon per minute by roughly 6.8 degrees Fahrenheit.

A 3.5 kilowatt unit raises one gallon per minute by about 24 degrees. If winter water arrives at 40 degrees and you want 105, you need a 65 degree rise, which that unit delivers at only about a third of a gallon per minute.

Why whole-house electric units need so much power

A whole-house unit that delivers several gallons per minute with a 60 degree rise needs tens of kilowatts, which means well over 100 amps at 240 volts in some models. That often calls for a new panel or service.

For this reason many New Jersey homes with gas or propane choose a gas tankless unit instead, which moves the load to fuel instead of the panel.

A realistic use for small units

A small electric unit near a distant bathroom sink or a workshop can give quick hot water for hand washing without waiting for the hot line to warm up. It will not run a shower in winter.

Treat it as a convenience, not a main heater.

Questions to ask before you buy

What is the flow rate at your winter inlet temperature, not the advertised rise? What size breaker and wire does the unit need? Does the unit have a minimum flow to activate?

Ask an electrician and a plumber to look at the project together, because the electrical and plumbing sides depend on each other.

A worked example

Say you want 1.5 gallons per minute at the kitchen sink in January, with 40 degree water and a 105 degree target. That is a 65 degree rise, which needs about 9.6 kilowatts, or 40 amps at 240 volts continuous, plus margin.

Compare that to the 3,800 watts available on a 20 amp circuit, and it is clear why such a heater needs a bigger circuit or a different fuel.

What to do with the numbers

If you want endless hot water for a whole house, plan on gas, propane or a major electrical upgrade. If you want a small boost at one sink, a point-of-use unit is fine.

In both cases, have a plumber and an electrician review the plan, because the pipes and the panel affect each other.

Rules of thumb for sizing

Add up the flow of the fixtures that run together, then find the temperature rise from your coldest inlet water to your target. Multiply flow by rise and divide by about 500 to get BTU per hour, then divide by 3,412 for kilowatts.

The result is the power needed. If it exceeds what your circuit can carry, you need gas, propane or an upgrade.

Hot water at a distant sink

The best use of a small electric unit is a sink far from the main heater, where waiting for hot water wastes minutes and gallons. A unit under the sink gives warm water in seconds at a low flow.

It will not heat a shower, so keep expectations modest.

Local notes for West Milford

Local conditions shift the numbers.

West Milford sits in the Highlands with long winters, so incoming water can be near freezing in January and warmer in summer.

Permits

Propane and gas

If you have gas or propane, a gas tankless unit avoids the circuit problem but needs the right vent and gas line.

Better options

For a whole house, see tankless water heater planning, or consider a standard tank under water heater installation. For one remote sink, a small point-of-use unit works for hand washing.

Tankless and amperage questions

Can I run a whole-house electric tankless on 20 amps?

No. They need much more current.

What breaker does a small point-of-use unit need?

Check the unit's label. An electrician sizes the circuit.

Is gas tankless a better fit?

Where gas or propane is available, often yes, with proper venting.

Will a heat pump unit help?

It uses less electricity but needs space and air flow.

What if my panel is full?

A sub-panel or service upgrade may be needed, and that is a job for a licensed electrician.

Is a gas tankless easier to install?

It avoids the large electrical load, but needs a vent and a suitable gas line.

Which code covers the circuit for an electric heater?

Circuits follow the National Electrical Code (NEC), which a licensed electrician applies, while the plumbing side follows the National Standard Plumbing Code adopted in N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15.
